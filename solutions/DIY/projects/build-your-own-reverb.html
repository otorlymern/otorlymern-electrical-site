Build Your Own Reverb

Acoustic effects build · passive pickup, externally amplified driver

← Back to DIY Audio & Video Projects

Description

A loudspeaker excites a spring or sheet of metal; a piezo contact microphone picks up the resulting vibration. Blend that return with the dry recording for a deliberately physical, colored reverb.

Source scope: Nicolas Collins, “Build Your Own Reverb,” Tape Op #76, two scanned pages (printed pp. 22–23). This guide covers the contact mic, Slinky and plate versions, driver alternatives, instrument resonators, and pre/post EQ. It is not a conventional reverb-tank electronics design.

The pickup needs no supply, but the driving speaker needs an appropriate amplifier. No amplifier schematic, speaker power rating, exact piezo model, plate dimensions, or calibrated decay target is supplied. Choose those from the equipment you actually have; do not interpret this as a specified commercial tank replacement.

Materials
AssemblyRequired materials / selection
Contact microphoneOne raw piezo disc, preferably with factory-attached wires; shielded cable under 10 feet; mono ¼-inch TS plug; electrical tape. Optional insulating coating and a DI/buffer for long runs.
Driver and monitoringExpendable loudspeaker, a suitable speaker amplifier, mixer send and separate return, headphones. A line-level mixer send alone is not a speaker amplifier.
Slinky versionOne Slinky or similar spring, gaffer tape, strong spring clip, stable boom stand. The article suspends the pickup end about 3–6 feet above the floor-mounted speaker.
Plate versionFreely resonating metal sheet, gong or large cymbal; secure suspension; small speaker about 3–5 inches in diameter; wine cork; epoxy; boom/clip; contact mic at an edge.
Tools / experimentsSoldering iron, solder, wire stripper, insulated joints, optional continuity meter; clothespins or removable damping; source/return EQ. Use eye/heat protection and provide cable strain relief.
Step-by-step guide

1. Assemble the contact microphone

  1. Identify the brass disc connection and the ceramic/crystal connection. Prefer a prewired disc: direct soldering onto the ceramic is difficult. If enclosed, remove the case without bending or scratching the disc. Disconnect an attached beeper circuit; the passive disc is the pickup. A third feedback electrode, if present, is not used.
  2. Connect the shield to the disc’s brass connection and the cable center conductor to the ceramic connection. At the TS plug, center conductor goes to tip and shield to sleeve. Do not join the two piezo terminals. This is an unbalanced pickup, not a balanced XLR microphone.
  3. Keep the shielded lead under 10 feet, as specified on source p. 1. This corrects the former 20-foot recommendation. With power off/disconnected, check the cable for shorts and continuity to its intended terminals; do not confuse the disc’s capacitive behavior with a plain resistive component.
  4. Connect to a suitable high-impedance instrument input or buffer. Start with gain down; tap gently and raise it. If a mic preamp is needed, use an appropriate DI rather than wiring the raw piezo directly to XLR. Connector shape alone does not establish input impedance.
  5. After a successful test, insulate the joints and cover the ceramic side with tape for strain relief and reduced touch hum. Collins also proposes coating the disc and first few inches of cable. Follow the coating product’s ventilation/curing instructions. This guide does not certify any coating as safe for underwater use.

2. Build and test the Slinky version

  1. Place an expendable speaker facing upward on the floor. Tape one spring end inside its cone. Do not use a valuable driver: the attachment and tension can damage it.
  2. Clip the contact mic to the other end and support that assembly from a stable boom, approximately 3–6 feet above the speaker in the article. Stretch the spring without pulling the speaker over or overloading its cone. Secure stands and keep the suspended spring clear of people.
  3. With the amplifier muted, pluck the spring and verify the pickup return. Listen on headphones so acoustic speaker leakage does not masquerade as processed sound.
  4. Patch source/mixer send → speaker amplifier → speaker. Patch contact mic → high-impedance input/buffer → mixer return. Do not feed the return back into the same send during first tests. Never connect a speaker-power output to a mixer input.
  5. Send a quiet percussive recording, raise driver level gradually, then blend the return with dry sound. Listen for the resonant tail after each transient rather than expecting smooth studio reverb.

3. Build and test the plate alternative

  1. Tap candidate metal objects and choose one with a useful ringing decay. Suspend securely with as little damping as practical. Protect sharp edges; do not hang a heavy sheet from an unverified fixing.
  2. Clamp the pickup at an edge. Epoxy a wine cork to the small speaker cone center, with enough protrusion to reach beyond the mounting gasket. Allow the adhesive to cure before driving it.
  3. Mount the speaker on a boom with a clip or tape. Using the same low-level send/return path, bring the cork just into contact with the plate. The purpose is vibration transfer, not pressing hard enough to stall the cone.
  4. Adjust cork distance/contact pressure and speaker/pickup position. Compare against the plate’s unaided ring. Excessive pressure or clamping can shorten rather than extend decay.

4. Tune the effect and extend it

ControlWhat to try / listen for
Spring tension and geometryRaise/lower the boom or change spring angle; compare tone and decay. Try another spring or driver.
DampingMove wooden clothespins along the spring; use removable damping/clamps on a plate. Record placement so a useful result can be repeated.
CouplingFor a plate, change the cork tip material or position. The article suggests different hard/soft contacts; avoid sharp additions where they could detach or injure someone.
StereoAdd a second pickup at another position, or a second complete spring/driver assembly. Two pickups on one object need not behave as independent reverbs.
EQ and blendEQ the send and pickup return independently. Piezo, speaker, and resonator all color the response. Judge the return in the mix, not only soloed.
IsolationMove the mechanical assembly out of the recording area. Use a suitable DI/balanced extension for a longer return run rather than extending the unbuffered piezo cable.

Drivers and other resonators in the article

A speaker can sometimes be bolted to a plate if cork height is correct, but its weight adds damping. Purpose-built contact exciters are alternatives, not electrically interchangeable parts: match their load and power requirements to their own amplifier documentation. The historical examples include Rolen-Star, Bass Shaker, I-Beam, ButtKicker, and flat-panel/stick-on drivers; their names are context, not current purchasing recommendations.

Collins also describes coupling into a piano soundboard with sustain held, an acoustic guitar/autoharp bridge, or a drum. These are alternate resonators, not required parts. Use only an expendable or permission-approved instrument, protect finishes, and avoid forcing a driver into an instrument opening.

5. Fault isolation and acceptance

SymptomCheck
No pickup signalTap the detached mic gently. If silent, check plug wiring, joints, cable and input selection before rebuilding the resonator.
Touch hum / weak thin returnCheck shield-to-brass connection, ceramic insulation, cable length and input impedance. Use a buffer/DI appropriate to the pickup.
Dry sound but little decayMonitor the pickup separately; inspect spring/plate contact and excessive mechanical damping.
Buzz / rattling / distortionLower amplifier level; check cork contact, clips, loose hardware, driver overload and return clipping separately.
Howl or runaway levelMute the send immediately. Remove return-to-send routing, lower gain and separate the monitor speakers from the resonator.
Completion checkVerify gentle taps are audible, the driver excites the object, a transient leaves a tail, and the wet return blends without feedback. Log object, support, pickup position, driver level and EQ; no fixed decay specification is promised.

Power off before soldering or altering driver wiring. Keep liquids away from powered audio equipment. Mechanical safety and amplifier/load compatibility are part of the build even though the contact mic itself is passive.
