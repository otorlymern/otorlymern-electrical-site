Peter Hillen · Synapse · May/June 1977

Vocal Synthesis

Vocal-tract model · modular patch study, not a Computalker kit

← Back to DIY Audio & Video Projects

Description

Peter Hillen’s “Vocal Synthesis” explains the Computalker approach to speech: analog sound generation and filtering under timed computer control. Its electronic vocal tract separates voiced excitation, oral formants, nasal resonance and fricative noise.

Source scope: the local PDF contains three pages, printed pp. 37–39 of Synapse, May/June 1977, not just the two pages previously embedded here. It is an overview with a functional block diagram and oscillator waveform drawing, not a component schematic, software listing or parts-level Computalker construction plan.

A modular adaptation can explore vowel-like sounds and articulation. It does not automatically provide intelligible text-to-speech. The article’s oral path uses three filters in series; a parallel formant bank is a different patch, not what Figure 1 draws.

Step-by-step guide

1. Follow the vocal-tract analogy (pp. 37–38)

The oscillator represents vocal-fold excitation, and its frequency and amplitude can change independently. The tract’s changing resonances shape that excitation into speech-like spectra. Hillen models the first three resonances with variable filters, while a separate fixed filter represents the nasal cavity. These are a simplified model, not a complete simulation of human anatomy.

PathAudio routing
Voiced common feedVocal oscillator → VCA 1. Its output feeds both oral mixer and nasal VCA.
OralVoiced feed + noise through VCA 2 → input mixer → band-pass VCF 1 → VCF 2 → VCF 3 → final mixer.
NasalVoiced feed → VCA 3 → fixed band-pass filter → final mixer.
FricativeNoise source → VCA 4 → low-pass VCF → final mixer.
OutputFinal mixer combines oral, nasal and fricative signals → suitable monitor/recording input.

2. Patch a reduced oral-path study first

  1. Keep monitor level low and mute nasal/fricative paths. Verify oscillator → voiced VCA → mixer is audible, with a slow hand/envelope control of the VCA. Use a suitable available excitation; any substitution for the article’s waveform is an adaptation.
  2. Add one band-pass filter and find an audible region with modest resonance. Add the second and third in series, testing after each. Serial band-pass stages may attenuate severely; do not compensate blindly with high gain or self-oscillation.
  3. Keep oscillator pitch steady while moving filter centers independently. Listen for changing vowel-like color. Then hold filter settings and vary oscillator pitch to hear the difference between excitation frequency and tract resonance.
  4. Record useful filter settings and gains from the actual modules. The article supplies no numeric A/E/I/O/U presets; do not present found settings as universal vowel targets.
  5. Add white noise through its own VCA into the oral mixer. Compare voiced only, noise only and mixed excitation at conservative levels. Do not confuse noise control with a second pitched oscillator.

3. Add the other branches and timing

  1. Split the voiced signal after its first VCA into a second VCA and fixed broad band-pass filter for the nasal branch. Add that filter’s output to a separate final-mixer channel. Keep its gain independent of the oral path.
  2. Split raw noise into a separate VCA and low-pass filter for fricatives; route it directly to another final-mixer channel, bypassing the three oral formant filters.
  3. Verify each branch alone before combining. Use a proper mixer for the summation rather than tying output jacks together.
  4. Try short noise envelopes and compare early versus delayed voiced onset. In Hillen’s account, stops such as B/D/G combine opening noise with prompt voicing, while P/T/K delay voicing. Fricatives and nasals require their own paths; liquids depend on changing articulation/timing. These are qualitative contrasts, not sufficient phoneme recipes.
  5. Return all levels to a safe baseline before each new patch. A sustainable result is an identifiable contrast between branches and articulation times, not an unverified claim of natural speech.

4. What the computer contributes (pp. 38–39)

A D/A interface translates computer values into controls for pitch, filter frequencies and amplitudes. The omitted software must coordinate their time histories. Hillen contrasts hand synthesis—analyzing a recorded speaker’s spectra and envelopes, then coding/editing parameters—with phonetic-symbol entry, where software interprets sound units, stress and timing. The scan provides neither the control data nor the software required to implement either method.

The phonetic example illustrates why written letters do not map directly onto speech sounds: homophones can share a rendering, context can change pronunciation, and transitions between sounds matter. The article’s assessment of contemporary speech quality is historical. It is not a claim about present-day speech systems or an invitation to copy a dictionary token into arbitrary modern software.

5. Musical uses in the third page

Hillen emphasizes control of timing and frequency separately: sustain a vowel beyond human breath, alter word/phrase speed without tape-speed pitch shift, reverse programmed sound-event order, or retune repeated phrasing for layered harmonies. The synthesis blocks can also act as a stored musical sequencer with no speech role. These possibilities depend on a control system beyond the block diagram.

The final example proposes replacing voiced excitation with guitar or recorded sound, while warning that vocal-oscillator wave-shaping circuitry would require modification. For a modular adaptation, use a documented external-audio input and appropriate level matching; the article does not show how to modify a Computalker board.
