Jerry Hunt · Synapse

Interactive A/V System

System design study · not a discrete DIY circuit

← Back to DIY Audio & Video Projects

Description

Jerry Hunt’s “Audio/Video Synthesis” describes a performance environment developed with David Dowe, not a generic audio-reactive video patch. Sound and image retain their own performance identities while a separate processor examines their changing control patterns and returns interrelated controls.

Source scope: four scanned pages, Synapse, March/April 1977, printed pp. 26–29. The source contains an essay, four system-level diagrams and an abbreviation key. It provides no subunit circuit schematics, component BOM, connector pinout, firmware, template data or complete algorithms. A faithful hardware replica cannot be built from this article alone.

This HTML is a standalone reading and system-analysis guide. Any present-day experiment below is explicitly an adaptation, not Hunt’s circuit or a validated modern implementation.

Step-by-step guide

1. Understand the compositional question (p. 26)

Hunt treats audio and video as extensions of composition and performance, rather than making one merely illustrate the other. His sound background and Dowe’s visual specialization inform a shared system with room for separate decisions. Brightness/intensity and visual orientation/sound distribution are possible correspondences, not universal conversion formulas. The history of variation is itself compositional material.

2. Follow the generating systems (pp. 27–28)

Audio: a parallel performance instrument

The audio system interrelates spectrum/formant, frequency, waveform and amplitude modulation. A plug-in ROM relates 128 switches to broad static program changes while dynamic controls remain playable. Its output organization permits parallel processing and re-entry, with delay used for spatial, spectral and frequency modulation. None of these descriptions supplies the missing oscillator, filter or matrix circuit details.

Video: a synchronous image processor

Primary video generation and modulation relate to the NTSC composite synchronizing signals. Camera and electronic images can be accessed together. Processing includes colorization, spatial/textural changes, edge/line derivatives, pulse modulation, chroma and gray-scale manipulation. Some subunits can be isolated for external patching. An optically coupled scan-conversion stage translates arbitrary-scan imagery for NTSC; selective digital storage supports limited image-feature work. Final images are encoded and distributed to monitors, recording or transmission equipment.

Do not replace this architecture with a claim that any audio CV can plug directly into composite video. Timing, picture information, internal control signals and external connections are different roles, and the article does not specify their electrical interfaces.

3. Trace the interactive cycle (p. 28)

  1. Select and scale parameters. Generator controls to be monitored are modified for electrical compatibility. Slope derivatives and synchronization help determine access to inputs.
  2. Preprocess histories. Nonlinear adaptive filters act per parameter channel; some channels also sample, hold or delay. This is already more than an instantaneous audio-to-brightness cable.
  3. Weight and combine. Programmable weighting and matrixing generate multiple signal branches. Variable-threshold processing forms parallel pattern codes.
  4. Store and evaluate. Codes enter recirculating memory. Dynamic templates establish variable goals within limits. The source explains their function but supplies neither numerical rules nor stored program contents.
  5. Return differentiated controls. Decoding and arithmetic generate separate groups of interrelated audio/video parameter controls. Program choices determine the degree of parallel behavior and goal relationships; firmware routing can reassign channels.

Hunt explicitly distinguishes this responsive exchange from a sequencer or randomizer, and considers simple direct interconnection limited. The earlier version of this page reduced the system to shared modulation; that is only a baseline comparison, not the article’s central adaptive architecture.

4. The portable derivative (pp. 28–29)

The smaller system supports works involving voice and image. Microphone/camera inputs feed selected voice/image parameter extractors; later alternatives use a performable voice emulator and image synthesizer. Extracted features control processor/generator variation according to performance definitions. Hunt discusses easier touring, setup and adaptation, while the larger system supports research and real-time performance. Neither diagram includes a buildable parameter-extractor or filter implementation.

Signal vocabulary

Sync establishes timing relationships. Composite video combines timing, luminance/gray-scale and chroma information; it is not merely a control voltage. PWM, PPM and PAM mean pulse-width, pulse-position and pulse-amplitude modulation. ROM is read-only memory. An adaptive filter changes according to signal relationships; the article’s glossary is descriptive, not a specification for implementing that adaptation.

6. A bounded modern study, not a replica build

  1. Draw independent known-good audio and image paths using finished hardware or software you already understand. Record each port’s role, permitted range, format, synchronization and output destination from its own documentation.
  2. Verify sound and image independently with cross-control disabled. Save this baseline and a way to restore it immediately. Do not connect unmatched analog ports to discover their limits.
  3. Choose one measurable audio feature and one image feature available in your tools. Write down what each represents, its sampling/update rate and allowed range. A hand-drawn feature/time plot is enough for a paper study.
  4. Compare direct one-way mapping with a deliberately specified history-dependent mapping. Document scaling, smoothing/history, goal limits and return paths before enabling them. These are design decisions you must supply, not rules extracted from Hunt’s missing firmware.
  5. Change one mapping at a time. Observe whether one performer can still act independently and whether the relationship persists over time without runaway behavior. Save the mapping, initial conditions and observations.

Do not open vintage CRT equipment or improvise mains/video circuitry from these diagrams. An actual hardware design requires additional primary documentation and qualified review. The essay’s references to contemporary technology describe 1977 practice, not current compatibility guarantees.
