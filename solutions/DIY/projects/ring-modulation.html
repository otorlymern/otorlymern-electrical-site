Ring Modulation Alternatives

Patch guide · source contains diagrams

← Back to DIY Audio & Video Projects

Description

A practical exploration of ring-modulator sounds beyond clangorous tones. The article uses control-wave shape, frequency, amplitude, and sequenced voltages to create tremolo, accents, and rhythmic changes.

Materials & complete parts schedule

This is a patch experiment, not a soldering project. The source is Stephan Bilow’s Beyond the Clang Tone: Alternative Ring Modulation, Synapse, July/August 1977, printed pp. 30–31. The original linked PDF contains only p. 30; the missing p. 31 and Diagram 4 were recovered and checked in the full issue, PDF pp. 32–33. No ring-modulator circuit, resistor/capacitor list, PCB, connector pinout or power-supply design is included. A scope is helpful for correlating voltage changes and accents but is not required by the source.

Construction, commissioning & operation
  1. Establish the audible signal and output path first.

    Using the compatible module manuals, power the existing equipment normally and verify VCO 1 can be heard through the chosen VCA/monitor route. Open the VCA with its proper control source so a closed VCA is not mistaken for failed ring modulation. Bring monitoring down again before connecting the three leads in Diagram 1:1.

  2. Test the ordinary two-VCO patch.

    Connect VCO 1 to signal, VCO 2 to carrier and modulator output to VCA. Begin with conservative levels within the modules’ ratings. Change carrier pitch while the signal pitch stays fixed, then change signal pitch while carrier stays fixed. Save an audible baseline before trying subaudio control.

  3. Characterize your actual carrier input.

    Lower the carrier oscillator into the subaudio range. Compare a slowly changing carrier and a constant carrier if your system safely supplies one. The article’s edge-accent and DC-rejection examples depend on its modulator/input coupling. If steady DC passes a scaled signal or reverses its polarity, you may have a DC-coupled multiplier; that is not evidence of a broken unit. Do not modify a module internally to force the historical result.

  4. Try a subaudio sine carrier.

    Hold signal waveform, pitch and gain steady. Sweep only the carrier rate and level, listening for tremolo-like variation. Compare the sound near sine-wave peaks and zero crossings with Diagram 1:2. The source attributes loudness to rate of carrier change in its setup; record what your own module actually does instead of treating the sketch as a universal transfer function.

  5. Try a ramp carrier.

    Replace only the carrier waveform with ramp. In the source’s behavior the long slope gives a steadier background and the abrupt reset gives an accent. Reverse ramp direction if your oscillator provides both slopes; note that clipping, AC coupling and source offset can alter the result. No numeric carrier amplitude or frequency is specified in the article.

  6. Try a pulse carrier and vary width.

    Compare pulse/square at the same slow cycle rate. Diagram 1:4 identifies transitions as accent points in the historical setup. Change pulse width without changing signal pitch; this changes the spacing of rising/falling edges. If your modulator sustains sound during the flat portions, document that implementation difference rather than assuming a wiring failure.

  7. Build the sequencer patch with equal voltages first.

    Remove the oscillator carrier and connect the sequencer’s stepped CV output to carrier as Diagram 2 shows. Leave the audio signal source and VCA route in place. Set four steps equal and run the sequencer slowly using its own required clock. Diagram 3 predicts no changing-voltage rhythm when the carrier stays constant; the source does not specify clock wiring or absolute CV values.

  8. Raise just step 1, leaving steps 2–4 equal.

    Two carrier transitions now occur per four-step cycle: 1→2 and 4→1. The source marks these as two accent boundaries. Listen at those boundaries and, if using a scope, compare the CV step timing with the audio output. Do not substitute the sequencer gate output for stepped CV.

  9. Set all four steps differently.

    Use Diagram 3’s final case as a relative pattern, not as numeric voltage settings: all four transitions can produce accents, including the loop boundary 4→1. The article relates the greatest voltage change to the strongest accent. Compare adjacent differences rather than assuming the highest absolute CV step must be loudest.

  10. Link pitch interval and accent strength (recovered Diagram 4).

    Split a single sequencer CV output using a suitable multiple or supported parallel output: one branch goes to VCO pitch CV and the other to ring-modulator carrier. VCO audio goes to signal; ring-modulator output goes to VCA. This adds one CV lead to the three-lead sequence patch. Use small adjacent pitch changes, then larger ones: the article predicts the following note’s accent depends on the size of the preceding pitch/CV interval, not simply its absolute pitch. Compare both ascending and descending intervals: the source says change magnitude matters, not sign. Stay within both modules’ input ranges; a passive CV split is not permission to join two outputs.

  11. Explore combinations only after the basic tests work.

    The source suggests combining two or three carriers. Use a compatible mixer or a module feature intended for summing; never tie oscillator outputs together with a passive multiple. The article supplies no summing circuit, resistor values or gain recipe. Keep combined levels within the carrier input specification and reduce listening level before experimenting.

Observation worksheet and expected behavior

Controlled test Source expectation / useful check
Sine, slow carrier Tremolo-like modulation. Compare carrier voltage shape and audible envelope rather than expecting a calibrated rate or depth.
Ramp reset A stronger transient at the abrupt return in the source’s system.
Pulse-width sweep Moving edge positions changes accent spacing; exact response is implementation-dependent.
Four equal sequencer CVs No transition-created rhythm; any steady feedthrough reveals the module’s DC/coupling behavior.
Only step 1 raised Accents at 1→2 and 4→1.
Four different CVs Four possible accents per loop; source predicts larger adjacent CV jumps produce stronger accents.

Troubleshooting without inventing a circuit

Symptom Check
Silence in every patch Verify signal oscillator, VCA opening, monitor routing, carrier input level and any ring-modulator balance controls using its own manual. Restore the baseline two-audio-VCO patch.
Steady tone rather than edge-only blips Check whether the carrier path is DC-coupled or the module is a four-quadrant multiplier. The historical response is not a universal specification.
Rhythm occurs only at some boundaries Read the actual sequencer CV values: equal adjacent steps make no CV jump. Include the last-to-first boundary.
Only clicks or harsh distortion Reduce carrier and signal levels, inspect for clipping and confirm both inputs accept the chosen signal/CV range. The source gives no universal voltage target.
Combined carriers behave badly Remove the combination patch and test each independently. Do not short two actively driven outputs together.

Keep a patch log with signal waveform/pitch, carrier waveform/rate/amplitude/offset, input gain, four relative CV settings, VCA state and observed accent timing. Those observations make the patch repeatable; the source provides no build calibration or bench measurement tolerances.

Source scope & implementation limits

Scope and attribution

The supplied PDF identifies the author as Stephan Bilow; that spelling replaces the legacy page’s Stephen Billow. Source: Beyond the Clang Tone: Alternative Ring Modulation, Synapse, July/August 1977, printed pp. 30–31. The original one-page extract stops mid-sentence. Its continuation and Diagram 4 were recovered from the complete issue, PDF pp. 32–33, and read alongside the original. All seven diagrams (1:1–1:4 and 2–4) are now inline. The continuation supplies the pitch/accent patch and additional carrier suggestions, but no component-level build or absolute voltages.

The explanation is written around voltage-change-responsive behavior and identifies a Steiner–Parker system for the sequencer example. Do not generalize “no signal at constant DC carrier” to every ring modulator. This guide reproduces the experiment and flags the missing interface specifications honestly: no component BOM, power rail values, carrier voltage limits, VCA control patch or complete ring-modulator hardware design appears in either article page.
