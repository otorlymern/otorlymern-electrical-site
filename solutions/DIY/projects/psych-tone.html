OES · Bench Release Candidate 0.9

OE(P)SycTone

A complete 9 V melody-weave synth build

← Back to DIY Audio & Video Projects

Start here

OE(P)SycTone is a compact electronic instrument inspired by Don Lancaster’s Psyc-Tone. A six-stage digital register circulates a repeating binary pattern. Three selected bits alter the timing of an audio oscillator, while another set of bits decides when its sound is heard. The result is a changing, repeatable melody with rests and timbre controls.

This guide is the build reference. It presents the OES 9 V CMOS reinterpretation as a module-by-module breadboard project, then shows how to move verified circuits onto permanent boards. The normal instrument is deterministic; the CHANGE input is an optional way to alter the repeating pattern. The original design’s six passive voice networks and envelope controls are expansion ideas, not prerequisites for this first instrument.

Release status: RC0.9 is intended for the bench. The digital architecture and pin connections are specified here; the analog pitch and voice values must be confirmed during a physical build. Keep notes about measured results so the next revision can incorporate them.

Suggested pace: complete one module per session. Do not add a new untested circuit to another untested circuit. A multimeter and careful notes are more useful than guessing when a stage does not work.

How the instrument works

The tempo circuit supplies a slow clock. Six flip-flops remember six bits and shift them along one place at each clock edge. An XOR gate combines the two last stages and feeds the result back to the first stage. Starting from the INIT pattern 100000, the circuit visits all 63 non-zero six-bit patterns before repeating.

Each register stage provides both Q and its complement, /Q. These twelve signals are possible sources for three tune destinations (A, B, C) and six PLAY inputs. A/B/C control three switched resistance branches in an audio-rate oscillator. The PLAY inputs are diode-ORed; when at least one selected input is HIGH, the audio gate opens.

Bench habit: build, power-off check, and powered-test one stage before moving on. Keep CMOS inputs at a defined logic level; an unconnected input can behave unpredictably.

1. Build the 9 V power and ground buses

The design uses one positive 9 V rail and a real 0 V ground. It does not use a virtual midpoint. Never connect a negative supply rail to a CMOS signal pin.

  1. Choose a breadboard rail for +9 V and another for 0 V. Connect a 9 V battery or regulated 9 V supply through a master switch to the positive rail. Connect supply negative to 0 V.
  2. With power disconnected, use continuity mode to check that +9 V and 0 V are not shorted.
  3. Power the empty breadboard and measure the rails. Confirm the positive rail is near 9 V and the ground rail is 0 V relative to supply negative.
  4. For every IC, install a 100 nF capacitor directly between pin 14 (VDD) and pin 7 (VSS). Keep leads short.
  5. Insert the first IC only after checking its notch orientation. Power it briefly; it should remain cool. Power off immediately if any CMOS part becomes warm.

2. Build and test the tempo clock (U1)

A CD40106 Schmitt inverter makes a relaxation oscillator. The capacitor charges and discharges through a resistance; its switching thresholds make the cycle repeat. P1 changes tempo, while R1 prevents the resistance from reaching nearly zero.

  2. Set P1 near its midpoint and power on. The LED should blink. If it appears steady, turn P1 toward greater resistance.
  3. Turn P1 through its useful range and note the slowest and fastest beat periods. A DMM on the clock output shows a changing average; the LED and tempo response are the simple checks.
  4. Stop here if the clock is unstable. The register depends on clean clock edges.

3. Learn one flip-flop before building six

A CD4013 D flip-flop copies the D input to Q on each rising clock edge and also exposes its complement /Q. Test one half before wiring the whole register.

Use three CD4013 ICs (U2–U4) for the six stages and one CD4070 (U5) for feedback. Every IC gets pin 14 to +9 V, pin 7 to 0 V, and its own 100 nF decoupler.

Tie U2 pins 3 and 11, U3 pins 3 and 11, and U4 pins 3 and 11 together as the clock bus; connect it to U1 pin 4. The data chain is Q1→D2, Q2→D3, Q3→D4, Q4→D5, and Q5→D6.

Wire XOR feedback and CHANGE

For U5 CD4070, pins 14 and 7 are power. Gate 1 takes Q5 at pin 1 and Q6 at pin 2; its output pin 3 goes to gate 2 input pin 5. Gate 2 output pin 4 feeds FF1 D at U2 pin 5. Gate 2’s other input, pin 6, is CHANGE. Install 100 kΩ from CHANGE to 0 V so it stays LOW when unused. Tie unused XOR inputs U5 pins 8, 9, 12, and 13 to 0 V.

Wire the one-button INIT bus

Join U2 pin 6 (FF1 SET), U2 pin 10 (FF2 RESET), U3 pins 4 and 10 (FF3/FF4 RESET), and U4 pins 4 and 10 (FF5/FF6 RESET) as INIT BUS. Add one 100 kΩ resistor from INIT BUS to 0 V and a momentary button from INIT BUS to +9 V. Keep unused asynchronous control pins grounded as listed in the table.

Prove the register before adding audio

  1. Attach temporary indicators to Q1–Q6, or measure the outputs one at a time.
  2. Set the clock to about one step per second. Press INIT. Expected state: Q1..Q6 = 100000.
  3. Release INIT and check the first states: 100000 → 010000 → 001000 → 000100 → 000010 → 100001 → 110000.
  4. Count until 100000 returns. The loop must take 63 clock pulses. If it repeats after 3, 7, 15, or 31, inspect the shift chain and Q5/Q6 feedback taps.
Do not continue until INIT and the plain 63-state cycle pass. CHANGE is an experiment; the unmodified weave is the reference behavior.

6. Build the Whistler oscillator and pitch branches

The original used a UJT oscillator. This build uses a CD40106 oscillator whose pitch shifts when three CD4066 switches add weighted resistances in parallel with its timing path. The behavior is the important feature; the exact analog response depends on component tolerance and should be measured.

The fixed 220 kΩ resistors limit how strongly a branch changes the oscillator. Begin with them as specified. If pitch changes are too subtle after listening, the manual’s first experiment is 150 kΩ; if the oscillator becomes unstable or jumps too far, try 330 kΩ. Change one value at a time and record the result.

  1. Before patching logic, listen to U6 pin 4 through a temporary 100 nF coupling capacitor into a mixer/interface at low volume.
  2. Turn P5 and confirm a stable whistle across most of its travel.
  3. Patch Q1 to TUNE A and turn P2. Hear whether two pitch regions follow Q1’s state.
  4. Add Q3 to B and /Q5 to C, one cable at a time. Write down the useful base-pitch and ratio-control ranges.

7. Add PLAY / PAUSE

The PLAY network chooses when sound is allowed through. One selected bit is HIGH for about half the cycle. Multiple diode-ORed taps make denser patterns of sounding states; because the source repeats, the rests repeat too.

  1. For each of six PLAY inputs, connect a jack to the anode of one 1N4148 diode.
  2. Join all six diode cathodes (the banded ends) at the GATE node. Add 100 kΩ from GATE to 0 V.
  3. Connect GATE to U7 CD4066 pin 12, the audio switch control. U7 pins 10 and 11 are the audio switch terminals: U6 pin 4 on one side, VOICE IN on the other.
  4. With no PLAY patch, GATE should be LOW and the sound silent. Patch Q1 to PLAY 1; roughly half of the clock states should pass audio. Add Q3 to PLAY 2 and another selected tap to hear a denser pattern.
  5. Try complementary taps to move the rest pattern. Always confirm diode bands point toward the common GATE node.

8. Add voice color, level, and output

For a first complete build, use three simple passive colors. These keep the core instrument achievable while leaving the historical six-voice bank as an optional later module.

This output is not designed to drive a speaker directly.

9. Power up and play the first melody

  1. Set LEVEL fully down. Leave CHANGE unconnected so its 100 kΩ pull-down holds it LOW.
  2. Patch Q1 → TUNE A, Q3 → TUNE B, and /Q5 → TUNE C. Patch Q2 → PLAY 1; leave other PLAY inputs empty.
  3. Set RATIO A/B/C near maximum resistance (weak contribution) and BASE PITCH near the middle.
  4. Power up and press INIT once. Confirm the register begins at 100000.
  5. Raise LEVEL slowly and set a comfortable tempo. Bring in RATIO A, then B, then C, listening after each change.
  6. Try other tap combinations. Change one cable at a time and write down the musical result.

Keep the permanent instrument modular so it remains serviceable and each subsystem can be removed for testing.

  1. Lay out each board on a 0.1-inch grid. Label component side and identify pin 1/notches before soldering.
  2. If using a paper component map, print at 100%, pierce the component holes, and mark the solder-side view MIRRORED.
  3. Install IC sockets first, then decoupling capacitors, passive components, jumpers, and harness wires.
  4. Before inserting ICs, continuity-check each net and check for shorts between power and ground.
  5. Bench-test each populated board on its own before connecting it to the other modules. Do not bury an untested board inside a chassis.

Panel and wiring layout

Give TEMPO, BASE PITCH, RATIO A/B/C, and LEVEL room to turn. Group the twelve Q/¬Q taps in register order, with TUNE A/B/C nearby. Put six PLAY jacks in a row. Separate INIT, HOLD, and CHANGE from the main patch field, and visually distinguish CHANGE because it alters register feedback. Route the audio output away from clock and tap wiring; keep a common 0 V reference accessible.

Optional expansions after the core works

  • CHANGE experiments: momentarily connect CHANGE (U5 pin 6) to +9 V around one clock edge, then remove it. Do this only after the standard 63-state cycle is verified. Never feed bipolar modular signals directly into this CMOS input.
  • External clock: add a buffered input designed for the actual voltage range. Do not assume a 5 V clock meets a 9 V CMOS HIGH threshold.
  • Additional voices: reconstruct the historical passive voice networks downstream of the gate, after the core works.
  • Envelope/VCA or CV/DAC: add as separate, tested modules so experiments do not disturb the melody computer.

Design lineage and references

The instrument is an OES educational reinterpretation of Don Lancaster’s Psyc-Tone, historically described as a six-stage pseudo-random tune computer with XOR feedback, Q/complement outputs, tune selection, pause logic, a UJT tone generator, voice networks, and tone shaping. The OE(P)SycTone build here replaces obsolete logic and mains-derived power with common through-hole CMOS and a 9 V supply, and uses a visible patch field, CD40106 Whistler oscillator, and three first-build voice colors. Grant Richter’s Noise Ring informs the optional CHANGE concept: it is an expansion point, not required for normal operation.

Safety and build scope: this is low-voltage educational electronics, but incorrect wiring can damage parts or connected equipment. Use a current-limited or protected supply when available, keep the audio level low during first tests, and remove power before rewiring.
